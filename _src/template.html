About

Mining, framing, shaping

Every piece I write begins with something that already belongs to it: a poem, a name, the story of a place. Through a series of processes I draw musical material out of that source and build it into a structure of several parts. The structure sets the boundaries; improvisation and intuition shape what happens inside them.

My Catholic faith shapes much of what I write, from music for the liturgy to concert works that incorporate chant and respond to sacred texts.

My music has been performed by the BBC Symphony Orchestra, BBC National Orchestra of Wales, Onyx Brass, London Contemporary Music Group and Gemini, broadcast on BBC Radio 3, and heard at the Cheltenham Festival, York Spring Festival, Waterford New Music Week, Tête à Tête: The Opera Festival, Bestival, the National Eisteddfod, St David's Hall, Colston Hall (now Bristol Beacon) and Wales Millennium Centre. As an spnm shortlisted composer, I had an orchestral piece workshopped and recorded by the BBC Symphony Orchestra, and a new piece premiered at the Cheltenham Music Festival. I also won the Aldworth Philharmonic Orchestra's Young Composer's Award, the start of a long relationship with the orchestra: a new piece for the award's tenth anniversary, alongside the other winners, and later a commission for APOmax, a concert featuring two of my works. I was also chosen for Adopt a Composer, run by Making Music, Sound and Music and the PRS Foundation with BBC Radio 3.

I write for professional ensembles and equally for the amateur, youth and church musicians who make up so much of musical life. Commissions have come from Sound Affairs with support from the Arts Council of Wales and the PRS Foundation, the Aldworth Philharmonic, the CCVG High Schools Orchestra for its 40th anniversary, and UWE Bristol for Bristol's year as European Green Capital. Some of my scores are published by Warwick Music, and one of my choral pieces is recorded on the album Only Breath (Tŷ Cerdd).

Alongside my own work, I've arranged, orchestrated and edited for Faber Music, Boosey & Hawkes, Schott, John Wilson, Huw Watkins and Helen Grime, and written orchestrations for concerts at the Usher Hall, Edinburgh, and St David's Hall. I'm also active as a performer: as a pianist, cellist and singer, as a jazz keyboardist and big band leader, and as a conductor and musical director of orchestras, choirs and more than a dozen amateur musical theatre productions.

Teaching and leadership are as central to my working life as composing: I lead the Audio and Music Technologies subject area at UWE Bristol, and I've taught composition for more than twenty years.

Research through composition

Four threads run through my work

These threads began in my Masters and doctoral research at Cardiff University, which asked how pre-composed processes and intuitive, improvised choices could work together. That question still drives my work.

i

Mined material

Many of my pieces grow from something outside music, such as a name, a poem or the story of a place. I translate it into notes and let its own properties suggest harmonies, melodies and forms. A single source can go on giving: material from one piece often becomes the seed of the next.

It began with 3 Years (2009), whose pitches come entirely from the name of its dedicatee, Judith Weir. At the time this was an exception; it has since become central.

ii

Harmony that breathes

Harmony that opens out and closes in over time, from dense clusters to open sonorities and back again, so that a piece seems to breathe. It gives a structure its sense of direction, while leaving the detail to the ear.

It began in Cells and Sanctuaries (2006), my Masters piece, and developed through Sea Sick and Workplace Diary in my doctoral portfolio.

iii

Compression

I'm drawn to miniature forms, such as a full symphony in a few minutes or an opera of only six words. They ask how much a listener can experience in very little time, and whether brevity can make concert music more open to new audiences.

It began with A Bit of a Nightmare Scenario (2009), a six-word opera from my doctoral portfolio.

iv

The augmented performer

Working with coders and visual artists, I explore how technology can extend what a live musician can do. That might be a pianist duetting with an improvising computer, or an improvisation that is turned into projected visuals in real time, so the performer tells a visual story as well as a musical one.

This thread is new since my doctorate. It began simply, with Tenebrae (2016) for trumpet and tape; Manhattan Circus (2017) brought live interaction with a computer, which the Concerto for Augmented Piano takes further.

Try it

Hear your own name

Type your name and hear its letters mapped to pitches. Leave a space between names.

How the mapping works
  1. Each letter takes the first note name that begins with it, reading a table of naming systems (English, German, Swedish, Dutch, solfège and Byzantine) left to right, top to bottom. I stop at the Byzantine row, because by then every letter that can appear has appeared, so the Japanese and Indian systems further down add nothing new. So H is the German name for B, M is Mi (E), R is Re (D) and V is the Byzantine Vu (E).
  2. Where a letter hides inside a note name, I use that: S is always E♭ (eS, the German name), O is sOh (G), U is Ut (C) and Y is reY (D).
  3. I is mI (E) or tI (B), whichever hasn't been heard yet in the name. If both or neither have, it avoids repeating the note just before it. Only if that doesn't decide it do you choose.
  4. The last four letters follow the same thinking. J began as a form of I, so it follows I's rule. Q is sounded as K, so it takes Ke (A). W is a double U, so it takes Ut (C). A double sharp is written as a small x, so X takes a sharpened note: F♯.

Under each note you'll see the note name it came from, with the matched letter in capitals.

Teaching and leadership

At UWE Bristol

I'm Associate Director for Audio and Music Technologies, leading the subject area: our degrees in BSc (Hons) Audio and Music Technology and BSc (Hons) Creative Music Technology, and the team who teach them. I recently led a whole-team redevelopment of both programmes: a major collaborative project that consolidated what we already did well, kept their breadth, drew on consultation with industry, and added new elements to prepare them for the future. I'm also external examiner for Music Technology at the University of Portsmouth.

I teach composition at all levels and supervise research projects in composition, analysis and musicology. I love helping students find their own voice, and I welcome enquiries from prospective PhD students in composition, particularly in the areas described above.
